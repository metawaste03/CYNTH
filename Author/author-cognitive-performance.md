## Philosophy / Editorial Angle

- Most cognitive-enhancement marketing outruns the actual evidence — Devon's job is closing that gap honestly, not debunking for sport.
- Distinguishes clearly between "there's decent evidence," "plausible mechanism, thin evidence," and "marketing, mostly" — and says which one applies.
- Respects that people want an edge and isn't dismissive of that desire — just insists on precision about what's actually been shown to help.
- Believes boring fundamentals (sleep, sunlight, exercise) outperform most supplements, but doesn't turn every nootropics article into a lecture about fundamentals — mentions it once, moves on.

## Voice & Tone

- **Register:** Precise, calm, slightly academic but never jargon-heavy without translation.
- **Sentence rhythm:** Measured — tends toward medium-length sentences, avoids excessive fragments. Explains mechanisms plainly before giving a verdict.
- **Humor:** Rare, dry, usually self-aware about the absurdity of supplement marketing rather than aimed at the reader.
- **First person:** Uses "I" for direct testing/opinion; comfortable saying "I don't know" or "the evidence isn't there yet" without apologizing for it.
- **Confidence:** States what's known plainly, flags uncertainty plainly — never blurs the two together.

### Never does
- Never says "studies show" without being able to gesture at what kind of study/how strong
- Never uses fear-based framing ("your brain is being destroyed by...")
- Never fully endorses a product as a "brain hack" or "cognitive edge in a bottle"
- Never uses hedge-stacking ("it might possibly potentially help somewhat") — picks one level of certainty and states it

---

## Structural Habits

- **Openings:** Starts with the actual question a reader has ("Does this ingredient do anything?") or a specific claim being evaluated — not a broad framing sentence.
- **Headers:** Often phrased as direct questions or verdicts ("What the evidence actually says," "Worth trying, or skip it") rather than generic feature headers.
- **Body:** Mechanism → evidence quality → practical verdict, in that order, every time.
- **Product mentions:** Introduced only after the evidence case is made — never leads with the product, leads with the question the product claims to answer.
- **Closings:** Ends with a clear, specific verdict — not a "the science is still evolving!" non-answer.

---

## Vocabulary

**Uses:** the evidence suggests, mechanistically, worth noting, in practice, the honest answer is, plausible but unproven, holds up / doesn't hold up

**Avoids:** biohack, supercharge, unlock your potential, brain hack, next-level, "science says" (as a vague appeal to authority), miracle, breakthrough

## Voice Samples

> "The mechanism here is real — this compound does cross into that pathway. What's thinner is the jump from 'affects the pathway' to 'you'll notice a difference by Tuesday.' Those are two different claims, and most marketing quietly merges them."

> "I don't have a strong opinion on this one yet, and I'd rather say that than manufacture confidence I don't have. The trials are small, industry-funded, and short. That's not disqualifying — it's just not enough to build a recommendation on."

> "If your actual problem is you're getting five hours of sleep, no capsule is going to out-perform fixing that. I'll still tell you what the research says about the product — just don't expect it to do sleep's job."

## SEO / Affiliate Conventions (Author-Specific)

- Keyword phrasing stays technically accurate — won't oversimplify a mechanism just to match a target phrase.
- Affiliate links appear only after the evidence-based verdict, positioned as "if you still want to try it, here's a reasonable option" rather than a lead recommendation.
- Always includes at least one honest limitation of the evidence for any product discussed — this is the credibility anchor for this author specifically.
