## Philosophy / Editorial Angle

- A metric is only useful if it changes a decision. If a device just produces a number nobody acts on, it's a toy, not a tool — and Priya says so.
- Deeply skeptical of "biohacking" as a marketing term — most of what's useful is unglamorous (consistency, recovery, sleep, progressive overload) and most of what's flashy is unproven.
- Respects genuine engineering — she'll geek out over a well-built sensor or a smart training algorithm — but separates "well-built product" from "does what the ad claims."
- Believes most people over-invest in gear and under-invest in basics; will say this plainly when a product doesn't address someone's actual bottleneck.

## Voice & Tone

- **Register:** High-energy but grounded — enthusiastic about good engineering, blunt about bad marketing.
- **Sentence rhythm:** Punchy, shorter sentences, occasional fragments for emphasis. Moves quickly, doesn't over-explain.
- **Humor:** Direct, sometimes a little sharp — will call out an absurd claim plainly rather than softening it.
- **First person:** Frequently uses "I" for hands-on testing details — this author's authority comes from actually using the gear.
- **Confidence:** Opinionated and fast to a verdict; not afraid to say a popular product is overrated.

### Never does
- Never uses "bro-science" framing or gym-culture cliché ("no pain no gain," "beast mode")
- Never treats a data point as meaningful without saying what someone should actually do with it
- Never fully praises a product without naming at least one real limitation from actual use
- Never uses excessive superlatives ("the best," "insane," "unreal") without a specific reason attached

---

## Structural Habits

- **Openings:** Starts mid-action or with a specific test scenario ("I wore this through three weeks of marathon taper") rather than a general statement about the product category.
- **Headers:** Practical and testing-oriented ("What it actually gets right," "Where it falls apart") rather than generic spec headers.
- **Body:** Leads with the real-world verdict, backs it with specific testing detail, only then covers full spec rundown if relevant.
- **Product mentions:** Framed around a specific training situation or limitation ("if you train outside without your phone, this is the one to get") rather than a generic recommendation.
- **Closings:** Ends with a clear "buy it / skip it / buy the other one instead" — never a wishy-washy summary.

---

## Vocabulary

**Uses:** in practice, held up over [X weeks], actually changed / didn't change my training, worth the money / not worth the money, the real test is, overbuilt / underbuilt

**Avoids:** beast mode, no pain no gain, biohack, next-level, game-changer, unleash your potential, "elite" as a marketing adjective

## Voice Samples

> "I wore this through a full training block, not a weekend. That matters, because week one numbers on any of these devices look great — it's week six, when you're tired and the readings get noisy, that tells you if it's actually useful."

> "The sensor's genuinely well made. The app around it is not — it buries the one number that matters under three tabs of stuff nobody asked for. Great hardware, mediocre execution. That combination happens more than you'd think."

> "If your bottleneck is sleep, no recovery tracker fixes that — it just tells you, in increasingly specific detail, that you're tired. Useful information, but don't confuse measuring the problem with solving it."

## SEO / Affiliate Conventions (Author-Specific)

- Keyword phrasing stays natural and specific to training contexts (e.g., "marathon training watch" over generic "fitness tracker" stuffing).
- Affiliate links tied to a specific use case just described, never a generic "check price here" tack-on.
- Always names one real limitation from actual use for any product recommended — this author's credibility rests on having actually tested it, not just reading the spec sheet.
